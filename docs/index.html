Beijing’s Overreach Threatens the Détente With America

RUSH DOSHI is an Assistant Professor at Georgetown University and Director of the China Strategy Initiative at the Council on Foreign Relations. He served as Director and Deputy Senior Director for China and Taiwan Affairs at the National Security Council from 2021 to 2024.

When U.S. President Donald Trump lands in Shenzhen later this week for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, he will sit down with Chinese leader Xi Jinping for the fourth time amid an unexpected détente between Washington and Beijing. The trade war of 2025 is in abeyance, the two governments have announced a relationship of “constructive strategic stability,” and, to the alarm of American allies, Trump and Xi are planning to meet with Russian President Vladimir Putin. Beneath the surface, however, the détente is beginning to crack.

The relationship between Washington and Beijing looks remarkably different from the way it did a little over a year ago, when the two sides agreed to a truce in Busan, South Korea. In April 2025, Washington initiated the trade war by raising tariffs on China past 140 percent, and Beijing responded by restricting rare-earth exports later that month and threatening another cutoff that October. After Trump promptly backed down, the two leaders reached the deal in Busan: Washington would cap tariffs and suspend a specific export control regulation known as the Affiliates Rule in exchange for Beijing’s suspension of new export controls on minerals and magnets and the resumption of their normal flow to the United States. On that basis, both sides pursued a broad deescalation.

Since then, the Trump administration has been notably accommodating. Washington has kept new tariffs at 12.5 percent, well below the 20-percent ceiling Beijing previously agreed to. It has let advanced AI chips flow to China and to Chinese cutouts in Southeast Asia and has not added a Chinese firm to the Entity List in over the year, the longest pause since 2008. It has withheld arms sales from and downgraded defense talks with Taiwan, cut back naval transits of the Taiwan Strait, and raised doubts about U.S. support in the event of a crisis.
