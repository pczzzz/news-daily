Rethinking Strategic Stability with China: From Coercion to Constructivism

Status recognition, that is, the position and identity of a state reciprocated by other states, is one area of negotiation and compromise that remains untested. Coercive diplomacy has not achieved strategic stability with China, but there are few alternatives in the public discourse. My intent is to provide some new thinking to address an old problem. The theory I present here is informed by academic experience, 20 years as a military officer in a nuclear career field, personal experience at a 2023 P5 meeting with Chinese defense officials, and ongoing affiliation with a long-term Track 1.5 participant. The sections below include a brief description of status, the critical and continuous role status plays in Chinese culture and national identity, and a status-informed approach with recommendations beyond coercion to build a new era of strategic stability. With a third U.S.-Chinese summit fast approaching, preparation for the upcoming meeting between President Donald Trump and General Secretary Xi Jinping is another opportunity to set the stage for the new era they both seek.

Status consists of hierarchy and identity . Each state holds a perception of itself and its position in the hierarchy, and other states validate that position and identity through patterns of behavior and social interactions. There are different organizing principles that generate distinct hierarchies within the international system of states. The distinction between nuclear and non-nuclear states is one organizing principle that results in a recognizable hierarchy. Furthermore, among the nuclear weapon states, there are additional perceptions of positions of authority and influence that affect the relationships between states in the hierarchy. For example, North Korea does not have the same global influence as the United States or Russia. The United Kingdom, France, India, and Pakistan are usually described as regional nuclear powers as opposed to global superpowers or great powers. Perceptions of rank within (or outside) the hierarchy generate interactions that reinforce the rank order in the group. This pattern of state behavior among nuclear powers is relatively stable.

Identity is a state’s self-perception in comparison to others . When others do not validate or reciprocate a self-perceived identity, the immediate remedy is to engage in identity-seeking behavior. According to social identity theory, identity-seeking behavior includes emulation, competition, or creativity in order to generate the desired social recognition . Just as individuals rely on others to recognize and reciprocate self-perceptions through social interactions or voluntary deference , states rely on others for validation as well. Social interactions among states generate patterns that reinforce identity throughout the larger system. The presence of a status deficit, defined as a state’s dissatisfaction with others’ reciprocation of its self-perceived identity and position in a hierarchy , results in status-seeking behavior if the state believes it is possible to reorder the hierarchy and/or reconcile its identity-recognition mismatch. China’s observable dissatisfaction with its status , its perception of the existing international hierarchy and its rank in the hierarchy, and China’s self-other comparison to the United States, collectively indicate a deficit China is trying to reconcile. It is helpful to understand why.

Understanding China’s status deficit requires the observer to set aside the outside-in perspective and examine China from the inside-out. Without an intuitive understanding of the Chinese mindset , it is more difficult to predict behavior, discern common ground, and mitigate one’s own biases affecting international relationships. Principally, understanding China requires first and foremost accepting the permanent and collective psychological injury the Chinese people have suffered at the hands of the “foreign other” for a century, and appreciating China’s unique needs, concerns, and historical memory spanning thousands of years. Understanding the past exposes how psychological factors and emotion affect China’s perspectives and behavior today. The Chinese Communist Party has used its grievance-laden narrative as a powerful identity-building and socially unifying tool to shape Chinese perceptions about the West, preserve the party’s legitimacy, and develop renewed Chinese nationalism. China has been doing this for decades, only much more aggressively under Xi .

Just as the West summarily dismissed Putin when he (truthfully) told the world his intentions in 2007, Xi has been sending similar signals for years, and they continue to shape China’s behavior . Recent examples from Xi include his speech to the 20th Party Congress in 2022 , the 80th anniversary address commemorating China’s victory over Japan , the summary of the May 2026 summit with Trump , and as recently as the summit of the BRICS economic bloc, comprised of Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa, on September 13, 2026 . In these speeches and in countless others, Xi emphasizes themes of respect and equality of all countries and nations, the importance of the past in building the future, the inevitable rejuvenation and peaceful rise of China, and China’s role as a global leader. It is well established that China is uniquely sensitive to status concerns , but now China is growing in confidence and demanding equality and deference as it regains its “rightful” place at the top of the world order .

That decision reflected a collision of two cultures. People who entered from civilian life built a different culture: continuous experimentation in place of pre-defined standards and protocols, openness to mistakes as the price of learning, and the habit of improving weapons in combat. For career officers, the question was usually one of means: which systems, in what quantity, and for which branch. The real question lies elsewhere: What game are we playing, and how do we imagine it?

The decisive dimension of the war in Ukraine is not where forces fight, but how fast each side adapts. A unit that updates its tactics and technology every six weeks does not cancel the firepower of a unit that updates once a year — a slower side can still pay in mass, as Russia does. What the faster unit wins is time: It sets the terms of every exchange for as long as the gap lasts. Mass decides how long a side can continue to pay, and speed determines how much each payment buys. I call this the Time Domain: a competition not for territory but for time itself — the interval between the moment a new capability reaches the front and the point at which the enemy’s answer to it works — won by whoever adapts faster. It is also an answer to the generals: The Unmanned Systems Forces do have a battleground of their own — not a place on the map, but a contest, the one their machines are built to win faster than anyone else.

When Russia launched its full-scale invasion, I returned to Ukraine from Stanford to help its fight. I worked inside Aerorozvidka , the volunteer community that pioneered network-centric warfare in Ukraine and helped create the Delta battlefield management system around a single conviction: push battlefield information outward, to those who act on it, instead of holding it inside headquarters. Later, I advised the first commander of the Unmanned Systems Forces and built the team that created the 412th Brigade “ Nemesis .”

Throughout this war, Russia has held superiority across every parameter a classical analyst would measure: personnel, artillery, missiles, armor. Under that asymmetry, innovation speed was never an advantage for Ukraine. It was a condition of existence.

The best commanders I worked with understood this intuitively: Tactics had to change faster, commanders had to build units differently, and time was the decisive resource. But they had no language to explain it to headquarters, no metric for a manufacturer, no concept to make it a requirement for doctrine. The analytical apparatus of military thought describes who stands where and how much each side has. It has no category for how quickly a force is becoming something else.

Military doctrine treats a domain as the environment in which operations take place, and NATO recognizes five : land, sea, air, cyberspace, and space. Each answers the question of “where.” But consider an ordinary grass field. People can play football, field hockey, and rugby on the same ground. The ground is identical, yet each game has its own team, its own rules, its own way of winning. The field does not define the game — the rules do. The pattern holds here, too: a distinct game with its own objective and rules, one a force wins on its own terms. Cyberspace earned its spot on the list not because it occupies a physical location, but because the contest there follows a logic the older categories never captured. This is what happened with Ukraine’s drone units. On that shared field, they were playing by rules nobody had written down, and they created a new game: a technology race detached from any portion of space, yet decisive in what happens on the ground. That is the game Ukraine built the Unmanned Systems Forces to play. American author Edward Luttwak described the search for a response as inherent in any military interaction: Adversaries constantly work to reverse each other’s moves. Every advantage has an expiry date. The only question is who renews it first. A single metric judges any decision, whether a commander’s, an engineer’s, or a procurement official’s, by how much time it buys before the enemy adapts.

None of this will satisfy a reader schooled in classical doctrine, and the objection is worth stating at full strength. Adaptation speed, how it runs, is not an environment but an attribute of every competition — present across all five domains rather than forming a sixth beside them. Cyberspace at least offered networks and hardware to stand in for terrain. But terrain was never what earned cyberspace its place — a lattice that rebuilds itself daily makes poor geography. NATO’s own doctrine has moved the same way: Since 2022, it has defined an “operational domain” not as a piece of the physical world but as “a specified sphere of capabilities and activities that can be applied within an engagement space” — the environment shapes each one, but none equals it. What makes this a domain of war, and not the truism that innovation matters everywhere, is the prize.

五角大楼战略资本办公室在过去两周内两次宣布将向 SpaceX 在发射业务方面的两家竞争对手提供现金。首先，10 月 26 日，该办公室宣布向 Stoke Space 提供 1.5 亿美元的有条件贷款承诺。军方官员周二透露，五角大楼为火箭实验室提供了第二笔 1.5 亿美元贷款。

斯托克航天公司和火箭实验室是首批获得战略资本办公室 (OSC) 大力支持的两家发射公司，该组织成立于 2022 年，旨在为开发“重要”国家安全技术的公司注入资金。 OSC 最近几周一直在大举贷款，向专门从事电池、无人机和半导体的公司提供了数亿美元的贷款。在太空领域，该办公室还向商业卫星跟踪公司LeoLabs提供了1.5亿美元的贷款，用于雷达装置的生产和部署。

五角大楼领先的发射提供商 SpaceX 已停止接受 2028 年之后猎鹰 9 号发射的商业订单，因为该公司将重点转向下一代超重型星舰火箭。为包括 NASA 和美国太空军在内的政府客户进行的猎鹰 9 号和猎鹰重型发射预计将持续一段时间。太空军与 SpaceX 的发射合同允许军方在 2029 年之前订购猎鹰 9 号和猎鹰重型火箭，实际发射将持续到 2033 年。

然后，如果 Starship 兑现了其承诺，SpaceX 计划让 Falcon 永远退役。 Starship 最终可能能够发射国家安全有效载荷，但 SpaceX 近期的首要任务是利用火箭的巨大货运能力来建设自己的 Starlink 互联网卫星和 Starmind 轨道数据中心。

自 20 世纪 90 年代以来，军事太空官员一直坚持确保进入太空的政策，五角大楼出资支持至少两个能够发射国家安全卫星的美国火箭计划。此前的支持以直接补贴、开发资金和溢价发射合同的形式提供，以使政府能够深入了解其发射提供商的技术和财务情况，安排其发射清单的优先级，并在某些情况下，只是为了让他们继续营业。

如今，太空部队的发射提供商包括 SpaceX 的猎鹰系列火箭和联合发射联盟的火神火箭，该火箭在过去两年中因其固体火箭助推器问题而两次停飞。 ULA 与 Vulcan 的麻烦迫使太空军将多项国家安全任务转移给 SpaceX。

其他发射提供商的目标是在不断增长的太空部队发射合同中争夺自己的份额。负责监督太空军发射采购的太空系统司令部现在预测，到 2030 年代初期，军方将需要其发射承包商名册进行多达 249 次中型到重型发射，这一数字是该司令部两年前预期的两倍多。

除了 SpaceX 和 ULA 之外，太空系统司令部还聘请了蓝色起源公司、火箭实验室、斯托克空间公司、相对论空间公司和脉冲空间公司，以获得参与部分国家安全发射的竞争资格。蓝色起源是唯一一家发射过能够满足太空部队要求的火箭的公司。 Rocket Lab、Stoke Space 和 Relativity Space 正在开发自己的中型和重型运载火箭，而 Impulse Space 则致力于太空推进。

五角大楼战略资本办公室的贷款旨在让斯托克航天公司和火箭实验室生产新型中型火箭，以满足军方的需求。

The US military is taking a new approach to prop up commercial launch companies as SpaceX looks to wind down its Falcon rocket program, suggesting defense officials see the workhorse rocket’s retirement as a potential national security risk.

The Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital has twice in the last two weeks announced it was sending cash to two of SpaceX’s competitors in the launch business. First, on October 26, the office announced a $150 million conditional loan commitment to Stoke Space. Military officials revealed a second $150 million Pentagon loan Tuesday for Rocket Lab.

Stoke Space and Rocket Lab are the first two launch companies to receive significant support from the Office of Strategic Capital (OSC), an organization established in 2022 to infuse companies developing “vital” national security technologies with capital. OSC has been on a loan spree in recent weeks, committing hundreds of millions of dollars to companies specializing in batteries, drones, and semiconductors. In the space sector, the office also provided a $150 million loan to LeoLabs , a commercial satellite tracking company, for production and deployment of radar units.

SpaceX, the Pentagon’s leading launch provider, has stopped taking commercial orders for Falcon 9 launches after 2028 as the company shifts its focus to the next-generation, super-heavy-lift Starship rocket. Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches for government customers, including NASA and the US Space Force, are expected to continue a while longer. The Space Force’s launch contract with SpaceX allows the military to place Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy orders through 2029, with the actual launches extending through 2033.

Then, if Starship is fulfilling its promise, SpaceX plans to retire Falcon for good. Starship may eventually be capable of launching national security payloads, but SpaceX’s near-term priority is using the rocket’s massive cargo capacity for its own Starlink Internet satellites and Starmind orbital data centers.

Military space officials have held to a policy of assured access to space since the 1990s, with the Pentagon paying to support at least two US rocket programs capable of launching national security satellites. The support previously came in the form of direct subsidies, development funding, and launch contracts at premium prices to give the government technical and financial insight into their launch providers, schedule priority on their launch manifests, and, in some cases, to simply keep them in business.

The Space Force’s mix of launch providers today consists of SpaceX’s Falcon family and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket, which has been grounded twice in the last two years by problems with its solid rocket boosters. ULA’s woes with Vulcan forced the Space Force to shift several national security missions to SpaceX.

Other launch providers aim to compete for their own slices of the growing pie of Space Force launch contracts . Space Systems Command, which oversees launch procurement for the Space Force, now forecasts the military will require up to 249 medium-to-heavy-lift launches through the early 2030s from its roster of launch contractors, more than double the command’s expectation just two years ago.

Alongside SpaceX and ULA, Space Systems Command has tapped Blue Origin, Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, Relativity Space, and Impulse Space for eligibility to compete for some of those national security launches. Blue Origin is the only company that has flown a rocket that could meet the Space Force’s requirements. Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, and Relativity Space are developing their own medium- and heavy-lift launch vehicles, while Impulse Space is working on in-space propulsion.

The loans from the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital aim to get new medium-class rockets from Stoke Space and Rocket Lab ready to meet the military’s needs.

亚马逊的 Ring Drone 似乎是该公司 Ring Always Home Cam 的直接继承者，后者仅在 2021 年 9 月作为“Day 1 Edition”产品暂时通过邀请购买，即使起价相同为 250 美元。

但 Ring Drone 上市销售前仍需获得联邦通信委员会的授权。假设这一目标实现，亚马逊的目标是在 2027 年 1 月 27 日推出这款无人机，客户可以通过产品页面注册电子邮件更新。

产品描述中的另一个警告是，在具有反射表面（例如“抛光大理石或湿瓷砖”）、没有任何功能的空白哑光墙壁或无框玻璃和镜子等透明表面的环境中导航时，环形无人机摄像头可能会出现性能下降。

Amazon is betting that many Ring customers will also want an AI-powered security drone and robot patrolling their homes or offices. But the company’s upcoming Ring Robotic products are also certain to intensify privacy concerns previously raised around a prior Amazon attempt to popularize a flying security camera.

The Ring Rover Cam and Ring Drone Cam are expected to reach customers starting at the end of 2026 and in early 2027, respectively, according to an Amazon announcement posted on November 10. Amazon describes the devices as relying on an AI Pilot system that uses each device’s cameras along with “specialized vision models, on-device sensors and cloud processing” to plan patrol routes while autonomously navigating around obstacles such as furniture.

Both devices also use that AI Pilot system to digitally map the floor plan of the customer’s home or business. This setup process occurs after the customer carries the Ring drone through their indoor spaces or allows the Ring Rover to do some autonomous exploration.

The AI Pilot can supposedly use that guided- or self-tour information to identify specific rooms and suggest patrol points near side doors, windows, or even sensitive locations such as a cash register in the case of a business.

However, flying and roving cameras with AI-enabled features could introduce new privacy risks for homes and businesses, on top of existing concerns involving fixed Ring cameras installed in room corners or as doorbell cameras.

Amazon’s Ring Drone appears to be a direct successor to the company’s Ring Always Home Cam that was only temporarily available for purchase by invitation as a “Day 1 Edition” product in September 2021—even having the same starting price of $250.

But the Ring Drone still requires authorization from the Federal Communications Commission before going on sale. Assuming that comes through, Amazon aims to make the drone available on January 27, 2027, with customers able to register for email updates through the product page .

Onboard camera capability includes a 148-degree field of view and 360-degree rotation with a 2K video resolution. Two LED lights also activate in low-light conditions to help illuminate the scene for the drone video camera.

However, the drone is limited to just 8 minutes of flight time when operating on a “new, fully-charged battery under controlled conditions” according to the product description. It comes with a docking station for charging.

Another caveat in the product description is that the Ring Drone Cam may experience degraded performance when navigating in environments with reflective surfaces such as “polished marble or wet tile,” blank matte walls without any features, or transparent surfaces like frameless glass and mirrors.
