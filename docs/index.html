Mike Lothian owns a docking station (or dock) that uses a DisplayLink chipset that had, at least in his opinion, subpar performance on Linux. The dock that he was trying to support is a Dell device that connects to a system via USB 3 and allows users to connect multiple monitors and other devices to their computer. It provides its DisplayLink Manager (DLM) software for Linux for the devices via an APT repository for Ubuntu.

The company only provides DLM software in binary form, and only for x86-based platforms. The software is proprietary, but the company does maintain an open-source, out-of-tree kernel module that is included with DLM, the Extensible Virtual Display Interface (EVDI) project. It provides a driver that's compatible with Linux DRM and allows users to manage their docked displays with standard tools used for display settings, such as xrandr or GNOME and KDE Plasma's utilities.

A short article explains why the company has not released source code for DLM: the software uses a proprietary graphics technology called DL3 to create a compressed stream of the various channels (e.g., USB, DisplayLink video, audio) over a communication channel (USB 3 in this case) and decode it again for output to the user's devices.

Unfortunately, the vendor-supplied software did not work well for Lothian. I spoke and chatted with him via Signal while writing this article; he said that the driver kept crashing under Linux, so he decided to use his Claude subscription to try to develop a new driver and support software.

The election of Democrats in Texas could affect the future of in-state tuition for undocumented students.

Photo illustration by Justin Morrison/Inside Higher Ed | dszc/E+/Getty Images

For the first time nearly two years, higher education immigration advocates are feeling hopeful.

Many have spent the last 18 months triaging a worsening situation for undocumented students’ access to higher education as the Trump administration’s Justice Department sued 24 states and Washington, D.C., plus individual universities, for allowing undocumented students who meet certain criteria to pay the same in-state tuition rates as their citizen peers.

Now, after a wave of victories for Democrats last week, advocates are hopeful that having Democrats in state attorney general offices, state legislatures, governors’ mansions and Congress may create “new opportunities to defend and restore tuition equity,” said Miriam Feldblum, president and CEO of the Presidents’ Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration.

“That change in leadership does not automatically change what’s happened in the court,” Feldblum said. “What new officials can accomplish will depend on the status of each case, the existing orders and their legal authority.”

新发现凸显了人工智能在年轻人关系中发挥的复杂作用。

人工智能已经越来越与年轻人的个人生活交织在一起，包括在大学期间，许多人正在发展自己的身份并建立有意义的关系。但随着人工智能成为情感支持和陪伴的来源，Rithm 项目的新研究表明，年轻人不一定要相信该技术才能向其寻求建议——他们只需要认为它比向他人倾诉的风险要小。

今年早些时候，该组织发布了一项研究，该研究对近 2,400 名 13 至 24 岁的年轻人（其中包括 500 多名 18 至 24 岁的年轻人）进行了调查，调查了他们的人工智能使用如何与他们的社交生活、情感健康和人际关系相交叉。

今天，Rithm 项目发布了新的研究成果，该研究成果基于对原始研究中 29 名年轻人的采访，其中包括 19 名目前在大学就读或已完成学士或副学士学位的年轻人。访谈主要针对最初调查中确定的两个群体：那些向人工智能寻求个人和情感支持的人以及那些与人工智能角色和角色互动的人。

Rithm 项目研发主管艾莉森·李 (Alison Lee) 表示，最值得注意的发现之一是年轻人如何权衡向人工智能倾诉的风险和向他人敞开心扉的风险。

“他们说的是‘当我转向人工智能时，我知道它会产生幻觉，我知道它让我困惑，我知道它会做出所有这些有问题的行为，’”李说。 “但与我求助于人类时相比，这些风险感觉更容易控制。”

English original

New findings highlight the complex role AI plays in young people’s relationships.

Artificial intelligence has become increasingly intertwined with young people’s personal lives, including during their college years, when many are developing their identities and building meaningful relationships. But as AI becomes a source of emotional support and companionship, new research from The Rithm Project suggests that young people don’t necessarily have to trust the technology to turn to it for advice—they just need to perceive it as less risky than confiding in another person.

Earlier this year, the organization released research based on a survey of nearly 2,400 young people ages 13 to 24—including more than 500 young adults ages 18 to 24—examining how their AI use intersects with their social lives, emotional well-being and relationships.

Today, The Rithm Project released new research that builds on those findings through interviews with 29 young people from the original study—including 19 who are either currently enrolled in college or have completed a bachelor’s or associate degree. The interviews focused on two groups identified in the initial survey: those who turn to AI for personal and emotional support and those who interact with AI characters and personas.

Alison Lee, head of research and development at The Rithm Project, said one of the most notable findings was how young people weighed the perceived risks of confiding in AI against those of opening up to another person.

“What they were saying was ‘When I turn to AI, I know it’s hallucinating, I know that it’s glazing me, I know that it’s engaging in all these problematic behaviors,’” Lee said. “‘But those feel way more manageable as a risk than when I turn to a human.’
