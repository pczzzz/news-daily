First, the good news: I have 133 microbial species living in my gut. That’s a collection that ranks as ‘excellent’ according to NirvanaBiome, the company in Germantown, Maryland, that analysed my stool sample. The firm based its rating on the overall diversity of my gut’s residents plus my levels of specific beneficial and undesirable species.

Revealed: how this common gut microbe protects against heart disease

But then came a second, less promising assessment: Viome in Bellevue, Washington, identified only 61 kinds of active microorganism in my gut and suggested that some of them were not performing optimally. Viome recommended dietary changes and offered customized supplements to improve my health.

I was intrigued by the results, but also flummoxed by the different messages. That’s not an uncommon response, says microbiologist Rob Knight , director of the Center for Microbiome Innovation at the University of California, San Diego. When scientists tested a handful of microbiome testing kits alongside one another in formal studies, they found wide discrepancies in the results. These differences make it hard to understand or really trust the findings from any given provider, says Diane Hoffmann, director of the Law and Health Care programme at the University of Maryland in Baltimore, who co-authored one of those studies.

The companies stand by their products. “Of course there’s variability,” says Kelly Moffat, chief operations officer for Cmbio in Germantown, which does the laboratory work and genetic sequencing for NirvanaBiome. “There has to be because this is complex testing.”

Momo Vuyisich, chief science officer at Viome’s lab in Bothwell, Washington, also says he was unsurprised by the degree of variation found by formal studies. Federal law affords colleges some flexibility to make academic progress requirements more lenient, but many aren’t using it, according to a new report.

About half of college students who fail to meet the government’s academic progress standards lose access to federal financial aid by the following semester. But some simple policy adjustments may help more maintain aid, argues a new report from the Lumina Foundation and Mathematica.

Federal law mandates that colleges and universities monitor whether students who receive federal grants and aid are making satisfactory academic progress (SAP)—measured by grades, pace and time to completion—as an accountability measure. However, failing SAP requirements isn’t always a reflection of a student’s inadequate effort or aptitude. Instead, it’s often the result of adverse circumstances, said Janai Raphael, strategy officer for access at the Lumina Foundation.

Take, for example, a mother of two who was doing well juggling childcare, work and college courses until her boss changed her schedule, Raphael said. She started missing classes, her grades slipped and by the end of the semester, she’d failed the SAP requirements and lost her financial aid.

“Now, to get back on track academically, she has to take more classes. But without financial aid, she’s going to have to pay for those classes out of pocket,” Raphael said at a media briefing about a report. “So what was initially a difficult semester has now become a substantial financial barrier that could keep her from continuing her education altogether.”

According to the report, 91.5 percent of institutions said students commonly say they struggle to meet SAP due to life circumstances affecting attendance. Another 85.3 percent cited health challenges, compared to 51.9 percent who cited insufficient academic preparation.

English original

Meanwhile, the Harvard Graduate Student Union strike entered its 22nd day Wednesday.

The union representing non-tenure-track faculty at Harvard University called off its strike Tuesday after the university doubled its raise offer, The Harvard Crimson reported . Though they are going back to work, members of the Harvard Academic Workers–United Auto Workers have yet to reach a bargaining agreement with university officials.

The university offered workers a 7 percent across-the-board raise that would take effect when the contract is ratified—up from the 3.5 percent bump previously on the table—plus a one-time bonus of 1.5 percent. This was enough to prompt union members to vote to return to work, J. Gregory Given, a teacher in the Harvard College Writing Program, told the Crimson .

“The offer that admin brought was substantial and is a significant improvement on what they’d offered last week,” Given said. “A significant enough improvement to call off the strike, in fact, but not good enough to settle yet.”
