人为引起的气候变化的大部分焦点都集中在化石燃料上，它们造成了我们的大部分问题。但土地利用变化也是这个故事的一个重要组成部分，约占历史CO 2 排放总量的三分之一。森林砍伐在这里发挥着重要作用，但排干湿地等做法也起到了重要作用。要弄清楚我们消灭湿地的历史究竟在多大程度上导致了变暖，很难弄清楚。但由于测量了当今幸存湿地排放量的新数据来源，有可能给出更好的数字。

麻省理工学院的 Jevan Yu 领导的一项新研究重点关注泥炭地——沼泽和沼泽等环境，有机物在这些环境中积累成厚厚的物质层。研究人员指出，这些巨大的泥炭储量中蕴藏着约 6000 亿吨碳，这比世界上所有活植被中所能找到的碳还要多。

由于这些区域永久被淹没，水中的氧气很快就会耗尽，将所有有机物的腐烂工作留给了厌氧细菌，它们的工作速度要慢得多，并且不会消化所有东西。因此，在泥炭地中，有机碳并没有转化为好氧细菌释放的CO 2 气体，而是其中一部分逐渐转化为甲烷气体。甲烷是一种比CO 2 本身更有效的温室气体，最终会被氧化成CO 2 。这意味着泥炭地需要进行一些权衡——泥炭地中的大部分碳都被排除在大气之外，但逸出的碳却以甲烷的形式逸出。

许多泥炭地因挖沟渠而被排干，使水流出，以将土地重新用于农业或其他用途。这会降低水位，为暴露的泥炭提供氧气，并敲响需氧细菌的晚餐铃声。甲烷排放量下降，而CO 2 排放量大幅上升。

研究人员利用最近测量泥炭地气体排放的研究建立了一个全球模型，估算水位下降时 CO 2 和甲烷的释放量。这两种气体对给定的水位下降的反应不同，尤其是 CO 2 根据沼泽所在的世界位置的不同，反应也不同。该团队利用历史泥炭地排水造成的水位变化重建，从 1800 年开始运行他们的模型。

表示 CO 2 和甲烷变化对气候影响的最简单方法是计算地球能源预算的变化，换句话说，是温室效应的强度。他们计算出，到 2020 年，CO 2 排放对地球表面的总影响约为 +83 毫瓦，而甲烷排放减少则对每平方米产生约 -9 毫瓦，总影响为 +73 毫瓦。这占人类造成的地球能源预算变化总量的 2.7%。 （考虑到所有这些数字的误差线，它是 1.8% - 3.6%。）

Much of the focus for human-caused climate change is on fossil fuels, which are creating most of our problems. But land use change is also a large component of the story, accounting for about one-third of total historical CO 2 emissions. Deforestation plays a major role here, but practices like draining wetlands also factor in. Exactly how much of our history of eliminating wetlands has contributed to warming is challenging to figure out. But thanks to new sources of data that measure emissions from today’s surviving wetlands, it’s possible to put a better number on it.

A new study led by Jevan Yu at MIT focuses on peatlands—environments like bogs and fens where organic matter accumulates into thick layers of material. Some 600 billion tons of carbon are held in these vast stores of peat, which is more than you can find in all the world’s living vegetation, the researchers note.

Because these areas are permanently inundated, any oxygen in the water is quickly used up, leaving the job of decaying all that organic matter to anaerobic bacteria that work much more slowly and don’t digest everything. So instead of seeing organic carbon turning into the CO 2 gas released by aerobic bacteria, in peatlands, a subset of it gradually turns into methane gas. Methane is an even more potent greenhouse gas than CO 2 itself and ultimately gets oxidized into CO 2 . That means there’s a little trade-off from peatlands—most of the carbon they have is kept out of the atmosphere, but the carbon that does escape does so as methane.

Many peatlands have been drained by digging ditches that let water flow out to repurpose the land for agriculture or other uses. That lowers the water level, supplying oxygen to the exposed peat and ringing the aerobic bacteria dinner bell. Methane emissions get turned down, and CO 2 emissions get turned way up.

The researchers used recent studies that measure gas emissions from peatlands to build a global model that estimated CO 2 and methane release as the water level drops. The two gases respond differently to a given drop in water level, and CO 2 in particular responds differently depending on where in the world the bog is located. Using reconstructions of water-level changes due to historical peatland drainage, the team ran their model starting in 1800.

The simplest way to represent the climate impact of both CO 2 and methane changes was to calculate the change in Earth’s energy budget—the strength of the greenhouse effect, in other words. They calculated the total effect in 2020 at about +83 milliwatts per square meter of the Earth’s surface from the CO 2 emissions, and about -9 milliwatts per square meter from the reduced methane emissions, for a total effect of +73 mW. That is 2.7 percent of the total human-caused change in Earth’s energy budget. (With the error bars on all these numbers, it’s 1.8–3.6 percent.)

Less friction Most of my infrastructure configuration lives in Git repositories, mainly in the form of Docker Compose files, scripts, and other configuration files. This makes it possible to use AI coding agents to work on my infrastructure in much the same way as they would work on any other software project. Whenever I want to try a new service, adjust an existing configuration, or automate something, I can now do so with considerably less effort. I’m thinking about repurposing the t640 as a “router on a stick”, though. I even took the opportunity to upgrade its CPU from an AMD Ryzen 5 2400G to a Ryzen 5 PRO 4650G and replaced the stock AMD cooler with a quieter Noctua cooler. Noise was the main reason I switched to the t640 in the first place. It’s nice to put some hardware I already owned back to use rather than buying an entirely new machine. I also managed to get both the CPU and the cooler for a good price through a classifieds site. Having some additional computing power available gives me more room to experiment. And there’s something quite satisfying about having a small box at home running a growing collection of services that I use in my everyday life. Building my own tools But it’s not just about self-hosting existing software. AI has also changed how I approach problems for which there isn’t an existing piece of software that does exactly what I want. Previously, I would often spend quite some time searching for a suitable open-source project, comparing different alternatives, and figuring out how to make one of them work for my particular use case. These days, for smaller problems, building my own solution has become a much more attractive option. I’ve recently built tools to automatically download invoices , e-papers or Linux ISOs . I can build (or have AI build) these tools in Go, my preferred programming language, and tailor them to my exact needs. They don’t need to support every possible use case, have an elaborate user interface, or be particularly interesting to anyone other than me. I don’t need to turn every little tool into a proper open-source project with extensive documentation and support for other people’s use cases. They just need to solve my problem. What I particularly enjoy is how well this complements my increasingly automated infrastructure. Building a small tool has become easier, and so has building a container image for it, deploying it, and keeping it running. The entire process, from having an idea to having something actually running on my home server, requires much less effort than it used to. Just because I can doesn’t mean I should Of course, there are downsides to all of this. Every additional service is another thing that needs to be maintained. AI-generated software isn’t exempt from bugs, security issues, or technical debt. And having an AI agent make changes to my infrastructure doesn’t absolve me of the responsibility of understanding those changes. There’s also the temptation to build or deploy things simply because I can. When implementing an idea becomes so easy, it’s perhaps more important than ever to occasionally ask myself whether it’s actually worth implementing in the first place. On the other hand, self-hosting is a hobby for me. Not everything I do needs to be economically sensible or particularly useful. Sometimes, experimenting with something new is the whole point. And with much of the routine maintenance automated, adding another service doesn’t necessarily mean adding as much recurring work as it used to. A somewhat ironic development? I find it somewhat ironic that AI, a technology largely dominated by big tech companies, is helping me become a little less dependent on those very companies in other areas of my digital life. I’m aware that this also creates a new kind of dependency. But the software and infrastructure configurations I create with the help of AI can continue to exist independently of the AI services I used to create them. And since I mostly use open-weight models, I have a wide range of providers to choose from. What I appreciate most, however, isn’t necessarily the time I’m saving. It’s that I’m more inclined to actually pursue the little ideas I have. I’m trying out technologies I’ve been curious about for years. I’m automating things that I’ve wanted to automate for a long time. I’m building little tools for myself. I’m tinkering with hardware again. AI hasn’t fundamentally changed why I like self-hosting. I still enjoy having control over my digital life, learning new things, and tinkering with technology. It’s just removed a lot of the friction that made me lose some of my enthusiasm for it over the years. And now, I’m having a lot of fun with it again.

I never really stopped self-hosting, but I became less inclined to experiment with new things. Maintaining what I already had running was enough for me. Over the last few months, however, I’ve found myself getting increasingly excited about self-hosting again. And, somewhat ironically, AI is a big reason for that. Less friction Most of my infrastructure configuration lives in Git repositories, mainly in the form of Docker Compose files, scripts, and other configuration files. This makes it possible to use AI coding agents to work on my infrastructure in much the same way as they would work on any other software project. Whenever I want to try a new service, adjust an existing configuration, or automate something, I can now do so with considerably less effort. In the past, I would sometimes put off certain changes simply because I didn’t feel like spending an evening reading changelogs and documentation or troubleshooting issues. It’s not that I couldn’t have done these things before. But often, the amount of effort required just wasn’t worth it to me. Now, even relatively spontaneous ideas often end up becoming reality. Trying things I always found interesting There are also technologies that I’ve always found interesting but never felt knowledgeable enough about to really make use of them. NixOS is a good example. I’ve always liked the idea of declaratively configuring an entire system; I even used it on my laptop during my Linux honeymoon phase. But getting everything configured the way I wanted was quite a hurdle. With the help of AI, I’m now making much more extensive use of NixOS on my home server. For my VPS infrastructure, I’ve also started building my own Debian-based bootc image (after thinking about it for some time). Having my entire operating system configuration defined in a Git repository and automatically building a bootable container image from it is something I find really cool. But it’s also something I probably wouldn’t have invested enough time in to get working on my own before. Even with the help of AI, it still took me half a day when I set it up a few months ago. Without AI, it would probably have taken me at least a week. AI makes experimenting with unfamiliar technologies much more approachable. I don’t necessarily need to understand every little detail before I can get started. I can learn about things along the way, ask questions, and gradually improve my configurations. Automating almost everything Something I’ve also finally managed to do is automate most of the build, update, and deployment processes for my infrastructure. A lot of the work involved in self-hosting isn’t particularly exciting. Keeping track of new releases, updating container image tags, building images, and deploying configuration changes are all things that need to be done regularly. I still remember what updating a service used to look like: bumping the version and adjusting the Docker Compose file on my laptop, pushing the changes to the Git remote, SSHing into the server, pulling the changes, and finally deploying them. Now, a single Git push is enough. If a push is even necessary. With Renovate, I no longer need to actively keep track of most software updates. It creates pull requests for me, and often, all I need to do is review and merge them. I can even do that on my phone while sitting on the sofa. Pushing changes or merging a pull request triggers the corresponding GitHub Actions or Forgejo Actions workflows, which take care of building and deploying everything. Of course, there’s nothing particularly groundbreaking about any of this. DevOps has been around for a long time, and none of these automation tools require AI. But AI has helped me put all these pieces together in a way that I previously never quite got around to doing. And the nice thing about automating these tasks is that the benefits extend well beyond the initial setup. I now have less tedious work to do every time I want to change something or update a service. 多年來，中國的小型煉油廠一直是伊朗石油的主要買家之一。由於美國的制裁，伊朗石油以大幅低於市場價格的折扣出售。然而，美國海上封鎖導致供應中斷數月，迫使這些獨立煉油企業尋找新的供應商。
