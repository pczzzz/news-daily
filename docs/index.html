Washington has spent years debating the role of artificial intelligence (AI) in nuclear command and control , and agonizing over the military implications of China-U.S. AI competition . It has spent far less time asking a different question: how will North Korean leader Kim Jong Un’s deterrence calculus change if he believes the United States is on the verge of obtaining a powerful, enduring advantage in artificial general intelligence?

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) – defined as powerful intelligence matching human performance across cognitive tasks – could further destabilize nuclear deterrence relationships that are already deteriorating. Weaker, non-AGI powers could become especially problematic. North Korea’s nuclear posture is already on a hair trigger. If the United States obtains AGI or AGI-like capabilities, Pyongyang could adopt an aggressive “use it or lose it” mentality because it fears a first strike that eradicates its nuclear weapons capabilities and decapitates its leadership.

Critically, the United States’ actual acquisition of AGI and the subsequent development of deterrence-nullifying capabilities matters less than North Korea’s perceptions of U.S. capabilities and intent. Because Pyongyang has adopted an aggressive nuclear posture and espouses such a critical, paranoid view of U.S. intentions, the situation could spiral even before AGI capabilities are operational. More attention should therefore be paid to how AGI could upset asymmetric deterrence equilibria, especially vis-à-vis Pyongyang.

If AGI takeoff truly unlocks exponential growth, a cascade of technological breakthroughs could spur unprecedented economic growth and expansion in military capabilities that some have termed agentic warfare . Under this scenario, an AGI-capable power could rapidly erode, if not eliminate, the second strike capabilities of a non-AGI power. This technological leap would amplify the unfortunate psychological tendency to become more risk-prone when facing losses, called prospect theory.

Accordingly, under AGI takeoff, non-AGI nuclear weapons powers, especially the weaker ones, will likely fear a near-future wherein they will be unable to deter the AGI power. They could therefore adopt a more aggressive posture. Of course, this isn’t the only rational reaction, but for reasons we will describe later, we think North Korea is much more likely to respond with extra aggression than it is to seek refuge through survivability, concealment, and arms control negotiations. Moreover, if recursive self-improvement delivers artificial superintelligence (ASI), defined as capabilities exceeding any human, the stakes will become much higher and nuclear decision-making timelines will become even more compressed.

AGI consequences, timelines, and diffusion remain a subject of debate , and key AI breakthroughs may be a business cycle or more away , but the balance of evidence suggests huge capability increases are possible. If scaling laws continue to hold, capabilities will keep rising: announced U.S. AI computing capacity is projected to increase by 239 percent this year alone, according to Federal Reserve staff calculations based on Epoch AI data. 世界最后的和平机会
The World’s Last Chance for Peace
Foreign Affairs · 11-13 05:00 · 65

佩德罗·桑切斯是西班牙政府总统。

在过去十年的某个时刻，世界开始接受战争是不可避免的。从乌克兰到黎巴嫩再到伊朗，侵略战争不断增加，带来恐怖、破坏和更广泛国际对抗的恐惧。各国政府越来越将战争视为需要制定预算和做好准备的事情。军费开支不断攀升，技术和军备竞赛愈演愈烈，越来越多的国家正在考虑是否应该拥有核威慑力量来保障自身安全。

没有什么比战争正常化更危险的了。它代表着对人类历史大部分时期持续存在的规范的回归，当时战争是国际体系的主要组织原则。武装冲突是国家解决争端或以牺牲弱邻为代价扩大领土的自然方式。

然而，二十世纪上半叶第一次和第二次世界大战的巨大废墟使现状变得不可接受。各国联合起来，努力将战争从合法的治国手段转变为非法行为。战争并没有在 1945 年结束，而是在国际社会强烈谴责之后的许多年才结束。

然而，近几十年来，各国政府对和平事业的承诺有所减弱。 2003年，数百万人走上数百个城市的街头，反对美国领导的入侵伊拉克，但战争仍在继续。它在错误的前提下进行战斗，违反了国际法，给伊拉克带来了破坏，加剧了地区不稳定，并助长了针对西方国家的怨恨和恐怖主义。这场冲突也标志着一个转折点。自此以后，随着地缘政治竞争的加剧以及对数十年来阻止战争行为的多边努力的漠视或冷漠，追求和平逐渐从国际议程中退出。

这条道路的尽头是另一场世界大战——二十世纪秩序旨在防止的灾难。随着可怕的未来迫在眉睫，各国政府必须重新学习反对战争的艺术。每个国家，包括我国西班牙，都有责任采取直接行动，限制他国发动战争的能力，并加强作为和平保障者的国际机构。

PEDRO SÁNCHEZ is President of the Government of Spain.

At some point during the last decade, the world started accepting war as inevitable. Wars of aggression, from Ukraine to Lebanon to Iran, are on the rise, bringing horror, ruin, and the specter of a wider international confrontation. Governments increasingly treat war as something to budget and prepare for. Military spending is climbing, technological and arms races are intensifying, and more and more countries are considering whether they should acquire a nuclear deterrent to guarantee their security.

Nothing is more dangerous than the normalization of war. It represents a return to a norm that persisted for most of human history, when war was the main organizing principle of the international system. Armed conflict was the natural way for states to settle disputes or enlarge their territory at the expense of weaker neighbors.

Yet the vast wreckage of World Wars I and II in the first half of the twentieth century made the status quo unacceptable. Countries united behind an effort to turn war from a legitimate instrument of statecraft into an outlawed act. Wars did not end in 1945, but for years after they were overwhelmingly condemned by the international community.

In recent decades, however, governments’ commitment to the cause of peace has weakened. Millions of people took to the streets in hundreds of cities to oppose the U.S.-led invasion of Iraq in 2003, but the war proceeded nonetheless. Fought on false premises and in violation of international law, it brought destruction to Iraq, fed regional instability, and fueled resentment and terrorism directed at Western countries. The conflict also marked a turning point. Ever since, the pursuit of peace has steadily receded from the international agenda amid deepening geopolitical competition and inattention or indifference to the multilateral efforts that for decades discouraged acts of war.

At the end of this path lies another world war—the catastrophe the twentieth-century order was designed to prevent. With that dire future looming, governments must relearn the art of opposing war. It is the responsibility of every country, including my own, Spain, to take direct action that constrains others’ ability to wage war and to strengthen the international institutions that will serve as guarantors of peace.

OpenAI said it has reassigned its robotics research team to the company’s main research team, reporting to Chief Research Officer Mark Chen, while the applied robotics and hardware teams have been reassigned to the company’s larger Applied organization, which reports to OpenAI President Greg Brockman. The change came recently enough that OpenAI had already registered a booth at CoRL, a top academic robotics conference in Austin, this week. Though the conference app and floor plan marked OpenAI’s spot, when Fortune visited booth #208, it sat empty with OpenAI’s logo nowhere to be seen.

OpenAI had a robotics team in its early days but wound it down in 2020, with cofounder Wojciech Zaremba later citing limited robot data and a more efficient path toward artificial general intelligence (AGI)—the human-like AI that has been OpenAI’s goal since its founding—through other methods. In particular, OpenAI became increasingly devoted to training ultra-large language models on vast datasets of human-written text. This is the approach that eventually yielded the company’s breakthrough product ChatGPT in late 2022. OpenAI rebuilt a stand alone robotics team in 2024, announcing a partnership with the startup Figure to develop AI models for humanoid robots. That partnership ended in February 2025, when Figure said it would develop its own robot AI. OpenAI has said it is currently developing its own humanoid robot, with CEO Sam Altman targeting a first public demonstration in 2027. The company spokesperson said that despite the reorganization, OpenAI remains committed to bringing general-purpose robots into the world. The reorganization reflects a bet that is gaining traction across the AI industry: that general-purpose models built for language, code, and vision can also be used for “embodied AI”—where AI models control devices that can act in the physical world, such as robots, driverless cars, or drones. This would obviate the need to have separate robotics teams building specialized AI models just to be the digital “brains” for robots. Researchers have seen surprising performance when connecting GPT-6 Astra to robots, directly using the general-purpose AI model to perform some robotic tasks. Guanya Shi, an assistant professor at Carnegie Mellon University’s Robotics Institute, reacted to the change at OpenAI, telling Fortune that he would interpret the move as a signal that OpenAI sees robotics as “no longer a special problem.” Instead, he said, they likely see robotics as a challenge that can be addressed as a downstream application of general intelligence—similar to the way the same underlying frontier AI models now power specialized coding tools, or tools for legal or financial work. OpenAI seemed to confirm this view in its response to Fortune. It said that at the start of the year, the company’s researchers assumed the best way to make progress on robotics was to build on its work on AI models such as Sora, a video generation model that, in order to create realistic videos, had to have some implicit understanding of how the physical world works. This discovery is what motivated OpenAI to disband the stand alone robotics team and move the affiliated researchers onto teams working on more general AI models. The reorganization also comes at a time when OpenAI is under pressure to rein in its costs and show that it is more focused on a few, core products. Last year, Altman told the company that it needed to abandon “side quests,” and the company has jettisoned some efforts—including Sora—in order to reallocate computing resources to more core and potentially-profitable endeavors. The company is under pressure to stanch its heavy operating losses—which reportedly totaled $10.9 billion in the most recent quarter—as it seeks to close a new funding round and prepares for a possible IPO in early 2027.
