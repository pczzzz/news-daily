Hello and welcome to Eye on AI. In this edition:

The biggest news of the week obviously is Nvidia’s decision to rehire Jensen Huang as CEO. My Fortune colleagues and I have been following that story closely and you can find links to our coverage in the “Fortune on AI” section below. The other big story of the week was Anthropic’s IPO, which we also covered in detail. You can find links to some of that coverage in the “Fortune on AI” section too. The latest issue of Fortune magazine also has a great package of stories on the Singularity—what is it, is it here, and what might it mean for business? I wrote the opening essay for that package, which also includes a look at the race to bring brain computer interfaces to market by Emily Forlini and a piece on the debate over whether AI could be conscious by Beatrice Nolan. You can check out the package here . While all the boardroom and stock market drama was playing out this week, a quieter sense of panic has gripped many AI researchers and safety experts. One key reason for their alarm is a report from METR, the nonprofit AI evaluation organization, assessing the extent to which AI is accelerating AI R&D itself at Anthropic. METR estimated that as of October, the pace of AI R&D progress at Anthropic had doubled, and that Anthropic’s use of AI to automate research was likely the major driver of this acceleration. It also said it did not see any reason to prevent further speed ups going forward. Now, METR said it was not entirely confident in its assessment. (It said the true figure could be anywhere from a 30% speedup to a tripling of the pace of research.) But it also revealed that Anthropic had some important restrictions around what METR was allowed to look at as well as what the organization was allowed to publicly disclose. METR was not allowed, for instance, to assess how much computing resources Anthropic was dedicating to AI R&D aimed at improving model capabilities compared to how much it was allocating to R&D aimed at improving AI safety. Also, in the public version of its report, METR was not allowed to publicly disclose Anthropic’s own assessment of how much progress it was making on fundamental algorithmic breakthroughs, as opposed to improvements due to better training methods. AI safety experts were roundly critical of Anthropic for having imposed these restrictions on METR. (The AI safety community has increasingly fallen out of love with Anthropic and increasingly view the company as reckless and hubristic.) Despite these caveats and limitations, METR’s headline figure—a doubling of the pace of AI R&D—has been enough to freak a lot of people out. Many AI experts now think we are approaching “recursive self-improvement” (or RSI), the moment when AI models can autonomously build the next, improved versions of themselves. Many experts think RSI carries a big risk that frontier labs will lose the ability to control models that are being autonomously built at an ever faster rate. If the METR report weren’t enough to frighten people about imminent RSI, Friday brought the news that a mathematician at Anthropic, Levent Alpöge, had used a not-yet-released model to prove the Riemann Hypothesis, considered by many to be the greatest unsolved mathematical problem known to humanity. An AI system that can solve the Riemann Hypothesis, so the logic goes, is also likely to be able to make substantial improvements in the architecture and training of AI models. It was yet another indication that frontier AI labs are on the cusp of RSI.

In response, calls for “pacing the frontier” by slowing further progress across all of the frontier AI labs are becoming increasingly urgent. But figuring out how to bring about an industry agreement on slowing down and developing a mechanism for enforcing a slowdown has proved a conundrum. Over the weekend and at least partly in response to the Riemann Hypothesis news, Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI’s chief scientist, wrote a blog post in which he put forth a relatively new idea about how to pace AI progress. In the past, many ideas around governing advanced AI development have focused on the computing power (as measured in FLOPS) used in a model’s training run as a proxy for that model’s capability. But this measure is increasingly seen as too crude. As researchers have developed more efficient AI architectures and training methods, and as they have focused more on data quality and post-training methods such as reinforcement learning, it has been possible to squeeze more capability out of a given amount of base model compute. Reasoning models in particular can exhibit different capabilities depending on how much compute they use at inference as opposed to during training. This all means that a model could have dangerous capabilities and not exceed a particular FLOPS threshold. Pachocki’s idea is to use something called a model’s perplexity as a more direct proxy for capability. Perplexity is a measure of how well a model predicts a data distribution. In the case of a large language model, it is how well it predicts the next token (the next word or part of a word) in a sentence. The lower the perplexity, the better the model is at this task. Pachocki notes that as AI models have gotten better at predicting the next word in a sentence, they have also gotten better at other tasks. So perplexity is a not terrible proxy for a model’s overall capabilities. Pachocki’s blog has gotten a lot of attention for publishing a graph that reveals the perplexity measurements of many of OpenAI’s models. He writes the GPT-3.5, which was the base model for the original version of ChatGPT in late 2022, had a log perplexity score just a hair below 1. It could string together a lot of coherent dialogue, but it was not capable at many other things, and was notably bad at mathematics. GPT-4 then represented a big drop in perplexity, coming in at 0.913. This was the first model that Microsoft researchers said exhibited “sparks of AGI.” Perplexity scores then continued to fall fairly gradually. (They even got worse for OpenAI’s o3 model, although the fact that this model used “chain of thought” reasoning made it more capable despite a worse base model perplexity—more on that in a sec.) But over the past year, since the release of GPT-5.2 in December 2025 (with a perplexity of 0.888), OpenAI’s perplexity scores have been getting better at a precipitous rate. GPT-6.2 Astra, released earlier this month, has a perplexity of 0.761—and Pachocki notes that this model was able to solve one of the seven Millennium Prize mathematical problems that were considered among the toughest challenges in all mathematics. What’s more, Pachocki’s graph indicates that OpenAI has an unnamed, unreleased model with a perplexity of 0.732. That means OpenAI has had a 16.5% improvement in perplexity in the span of 12 months, whereas before GPT-5.2, it had taken two years to generate an 11.6% improvement in perplexity scores. So again, that’s an indicator of accelerating progress. Pachocki thinks the pace of this progress could be slowed by having every frontier lab reveal their current base model perplexity scores and then getting them to agree not to improve them below a certain commonly-agreed threshold. He says the labs should also agree on what kind of safety measures would be needed before any lab could train a model with a lower perplexity. “I’d like an industry standard that specifies a first limit on development, measured by some key metric we can track across labs, and what concretely we’d need to achieve before going beyond it,” he wrote.

Judging by the response on X, a lot of people liked Pachocki’s idea. Pachocki’s boss Sam Altman unsurprisingly endorsed the idea calling it “the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while.” Mustafa Suleyman, Microsoft’s AI CEO, said “we fully support OpenAI’s transparency initiative” and even published the perplexity of Microsoft’s own latest model MAI-2 Base using the same test set OpenAI used. Nathan Calvin, the chief strategy officer at AI policy nonprofit Encode AI, called it “a great post” and said it “could be one important component of comparing capabilities across developers.” Janet Egan, director of emerging technology policy at the Institute for Progress, called on Anthropic and Google DeepMind to publish the same kind of perplexity figures OpenAI had. But, that said, even Pachocki acknowledged his idea had “challenges” as he called them. For one thing, perplexity does not provide an apples-to-apples comparison across AI models unless they are each evaluated on the same set of held-out test data (meaning the AI model has not seen that data before in training prior to the test.) It’s not clear how easily such a test set could be created at this point since a lot of the world’s human-created data has been hoovered up and used for model training already. These days a lot of model capability comes from post-training, where a model that has initially been trained on next token prediction (which is what the perplexity score is based on), is then further trained through reinforcement learning to maximize some specific goal, such as doing well on hard math and coding problems, or writing legal documents, or acting as a friendly chatbot. The perplexity of the base model might not tell you much about which dangerous capabilities can be elicited from the model with the right post-training recipe. There is also the issue that giving varying amounts of compute to a reasoning model at the time of inference can produce varying performance on different tasks. I asked my friend Robert Trager, who heads the AI Governance Initiative at the Oxford Martin School at the University of Oxford, what he thought of Pachocki’s idea. He gave it a mixed review. On the one hand, he said, everyone likes the idea of a single, concrete metric. On the other, imposing a limit on based model perplexity would likely mean “the locus of competition shifts to post-training.” Trager noted that, as far as we know, frontier AI companies are currently using more of their computing power on post-training than on pre-training and they are doing so for a reason—namely that post-training matters a lot to what the resulting model can do. So it is not clear that a limit imposed on pretraining would prevent companies from building dangerous AI. He also said that there might be a problem where a highly-capable AI model recognized the perplexity test as an evaluation and deliberately underperformed. (It is also possible, he said, that an AI lab might deliberately include data in the pretraining data set that would elicit this kind of sandbagging when the model is tested on the perplexity test—something akin to Volkswagen programming its cars to readout false data on emissions tests.) At the end of the day, Trager said, having a metric, or even a set of metrics, is only one part of the puzzle of building a pacing mechanism. There also has to be some kind of enforcement to prevent AI companies from violating the agreed upon standards. And while a government licensing regime might be a good way to prevent a dangerous model from being released, it’s less clear how to address the danger from companies building dangerous models internally. If an AI is truly dangerous, then by the time it gets to the perplexity testing stage, it might already be too late. Perhaps Miles Brundage, the former OpenAI head of policy research who now leads AVERI, the AI auditing nonprofit, summed up the feelings of many in AI safety research when he said on X that an immediate proposal like Pachocki’s, even if imperfect, was probably better than nothing. If the METR report on Anthropic and the Riemann Hypothesis news are any indication, there isn’t much time to act.
